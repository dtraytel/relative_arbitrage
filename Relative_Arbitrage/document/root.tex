\title{The Relative Arbitrage Problem under Eigenvalue Lower Bounds}
\author{Dmitriy Traytel}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  A formalisation of Theorem 1.1 of Lai, Shkolnikov and
  Soner~\cite{LaiShkolnikovSoner}.  For a compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$
  and $1 \le k < n$, let $v(x)$ be the supremum, over all martingale laws
  started at $x$ whose instantaneous covariation has its $k$ smallest
  eigenvalues bounded below and its largest bounded above, of the essential
  infimum of the exit time from $K$.  The theorem states that $v$ is the
  unique bounded upper semicontinuous viscosity solution of the degenerate
  elliptic equation $F(\nabla v, \nabla^{2} v) = 1$ on $K$, with the zero
  boundary condition of the paper's Definition 3.1.

  All five clauses are proved.  Finiteness and upper semicontinuity of $v$
  come from tightness and sequential compactness of the class of admissible
  laws, which is shown closed under weak limits by carrying the covariation as
  a second, uniformly Lipschitz path coordinate.  The viscosity property
  rests on the dynamic programming principle of the paper's Proposition 2.4,
  which the paper defers to Larsson and Ruf~\cite{LarssonRuf} and which is
  proved here in full, at a deterministic time and at a stopping time: the
  lower half by pasting an optimal continuation onto a stopped law, the upper
  half by conditioning through a regular conditional distribution.  The
  measurable selection of an optimizer, for which Larsson and Ruf cite
  Bertsekas and Shreve~\cite{BertsekasShreve}, is proved here by a greedy
  nested bisection.  Uniqueness is the comparison principle of the paper's
  Theorem 4.2, built on the Crandall--Ishii theorem on
  sums~\cite{CrandallIshiiLions}.  Example 3.1, the explicit value on a ball,
  is derived in closed form for every $k$.

  Two remarks on the statement.  The zero boundary condition is a viscosity
  condition and not the pointwise identity $v = 0$ on $\partial K$, which is
  false in general: by the paper's own Lemma 5.3, the cube in $\mathbb{R}^3$
  with $k = 2$ has $v > 0$ on the open two-dimensional faces of its boundary.
  And continuity of $v$ is not part of the theorem; uniqueness holds among
  bounded upper semicontinuous solutions.

  The reusable parts of the development are separate entries: the
  differentiability theory behind comparison, the construction of Wiener
  measure, the martingale sampling toolbox, and the path-space tightness
  machinery.
\end{abstract}
